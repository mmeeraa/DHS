Maps can help us understand how people and places are connected. For this assignment, I chose Morocco as the country I will be analyzing, as I am familiar with it as a childhood vacation destination. What I know about the country is very limited since I would only be around Rabat most of the time and occasionally take road trips to Casablanca. This made me curious to know more about their modes of transportation to other parts of Morocco, as the places I have been to are tourist spots, and I am intrigued to know beyond that. How do people commute within the cities, travel to villages, and connect with the rest of Morocco? To analyze this, I used three GeoName features, including roads, airports, and railroad stations. Before gathering and studying the data, I expected to find hundreds of roads widely distributed across Morocco because that was the main form of transporation I experienced and saw, while I expected the airports to have very few points, as there was only one main one in Casablanca that offered direct flights from Abu Dhabi rather than in Rabat, Tangier, or other cities. For railroad stations, I only knew about their existence, but not how far they could go across Morocco and where they exactly are. Thus, by mapping these three features, I wanted to see how the people in Morocco and the places are connected by studying and comparing the different modes of transportation and how they are distributed across the country.

To examine the features across Morocco, I used the GeoNames dataset, which contains 153,872 rows. Each row represents a geographical feature that is recorded in GeoNames, and for each entry it has the GeoName ID, the name of the place, latitude, longitude, feature code, country code, and more, but also the modification date. This highlights that the dataset is updated with modifications primarily made since 2021 and the most recent being September 2nd, 2026. For the three features, I chose RD, which represents roads; AIRP, which represents airports; and RSTN, which represents railroad stations. The reason why I chose these features is because I felt that they were the three that best captured mobility and the network of movement in Morocco.

Looking at the map, I notice some patterns emerge in how Morocco's roads, airports, and railroad stations are distributed, where there are areas with distinct clusters but also areas that are more sparse. Starting with the roads, I noticed that there is a dense network across Northern Morocco, especially in the central highways and interestingly in areas next to Khouribga **(See Figure 1)**. When I researched about the place, I found out that it is known for its phosphate reserves and mining and is known as an industrial hub. Because of this, truck and service roads are needed to haul the resources gathered. Furthermore, the network connects Marrakesh with Safi, El Jadida, Khourbiga, Casablanca, and other smaller neighboring towns in between. It also is connected to places further up north near Fez and even further around El Aioun Sidi Mellouk. However, there are still several areas on the map that show no data of roads at all, especially in the middle and southern parts of Morocco, even though I personally know there are like in Rabat, and I can tell by just seeing the road lines on the map as well.

Moving on to airports, they are widely distributed across Morocco and seem to have complete data at first glance, as there are 31 points. However, when I researched how many there are, including airbases, it turned out there are only 28. I went through all the data with the label 'AIRP' and compared it to a Wikipedia list of the airports. I found out that M’rizig Airport is a local airfield that is not officially counted, but GeoNames has the data stored as an airport for it. The most interesting one was finding El Kelaa ’Des Srarhna mislabelled as an airport **(See Figure 2)**, when there is not one there. I even looked at a satellite image of those exact coordinates on Google Maps but found no sign of an airstrip. Lastly, "Tourisme" was wrongfully labeled as an airport and probably accidentally written in as well, as the coordinates pasted in Google Maps revealed a cemetery instead.

Apart from the errors I found, it was still quite interesting to see that all the airport locations have been recognized, even the small, local ones and the ones that further extend to the south of Morocco. This makes airports the only transportation feature with a complete dataset.

Finally, the railroad stations show a clear, concentrated pattern that connects Marrakesh to Safi to Casablanca to Rabat to Tangier to Meknes to Oujda to Bourafa and everything in between. I could tell it was the railroad stations immediately, as they followed a close line like railroad routes do. My initial thought was that the absence of railroad stations in southern Morocco was due to missing data; however, when I compared the map to an actual railroad map online, the patterns matched almost exactly **(See Figure 3)**. This suggests that the lack of railroad stations in the rest of Morocco is not due to the missing GeoNames data but likely due to those areas dominated by The Sahara Desert and mountains, as well as low population, where, financially speaking, railroads would be more connected to economic hubs than small towns, for instance.

While the map highlights the major transportation networks in Morocco, when we look closely, there are gaps between the recorded data and actual reality. Even though industrial hubs like Khouribga explain why some areas have dense road networks, it still does not answer the question as to why the road points are clustered in just that northern area when there are definitely many more roads across the entirety of Morocco. At first, I had the assumption that the roads might be underdeveloped in those regions or the areas are just not as well represented. However, that does not add up because Rabat, which is the capital and an economically developed city, does not have roads represented either. This is related to Kitchin and Lauriault's discussion of Critical Data Studies that argues that data should not be considered objective but questioned about how it was collected and represented, as it is actively constructed by society. In the case of the missing road data, it does not mean that there are only roads in the northern part of Morocco, but that they are the only ones mapped in that dataset.

GeoNames is a data assemblage because the datasets are produced and maintained by different people and sources. The results of the map I generated were due to the choices of the people organizing and collecting the data for it. On the GeoNames team webpage, Morocco has Karim Chraibi as an ambassador, which shows that Morocco has people maintaining its data, as proven by the recent updates in the dataset previously mentioned. After looking through the ‘About’ and ‘Export’ pages of GeoNames, I found out that the sources of the data come from over a hundred different ones and that by having an account, users can edit and add places in the datasets. This means that the data collection can depend on the local contributors, which could explain why some of the road data have more detail, like in Khouribga, than other areas with little to no data. 

In conclusion, this project highlighted the modes of transportation and how the people and places are connected in Morocco using the GeoNames data. The maps showed clear distinctions in representation among roads, railroad routes, and airports: road data was missing, railroad stations were in a clear clustered path connecting the northern economic hubs, and airports were the only widely distributed data but contained inaccuracies. This taught me that maps do not always represent what exists in the real world and that their collection should be questioned and not taken at face value. 

In terms of transferability, I can definitely see me using what I learned in this project in a course I am taking now called Research Design and Causality. The professor in that class always reminds us to be cynical when reading research articles and to question their motives because a lot of the findings can be filtered in a way to display statistically significant data in order to get their articles published in the top journals. This I think is very closely related to Kitchin and Lauriault's Critical Data Studies paper. When I start making my research design, I will think about how the data was collected and represented instead of accepting the data as complete. It applies to every and all my future research papers that I will conduct and need to gather data for.
